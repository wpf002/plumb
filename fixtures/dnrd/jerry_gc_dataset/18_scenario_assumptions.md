
GENERAL CONTRACTOR JERRY — SCENARIO MODELING ASSUMPTIONS (computed from actual dataset)

Scenario 1: "Should I hire another crew (2 more field carpenters + a truck)?"
  Fully burdened cost of 2 additional field carpenters: ~$158,600/yr, plus a ~$38,000 truck
  (financed, ~$650/mo all-in). Current field crew (9 people) produced $539,447/employee in the best year
  (2023) -- a realistic ceiling. Two more field employees imply roughly $1,078,894 of added annual
  revenue capacity IF sales/estimating can generate that much additional signed work (today's weighted pipeline is
  $879,750 and signed backlog is $284,252 -- not yet
  enough, on its own, to fully utilize 2 more people year-round). Recommendation implied by the data: the hire is
  affordable, but should follow -- not precede -- winning 1-2 more signed remodels or a custom home, given how thin the
  current pipeline is relative to the incremental capacity.

Scenario 2: "What happens if materials prices rise 10%?"
  2025 materials cost was $862,795 (27.3% of revenue). A 10% increase
  applied to in-progress and newly-signed fixed-price contracts (where the increase cannot be passed through) would add
  approximately $86,279 of cost in a year at 2025's volume, which would have reduced 2025 EBITDA
  ($96,296) by 90% if fully absorbed --
  a materially larger impact than it would have been in the already-thin 2024 year. This is the single clearest argument
  for either shorter price-validity windows on estimates or a materials-escalation clause in new contracts, especially
  on the 9-15 month custom-home jobs where price exposure compounds over a much longer window than a 1-3 month remodel.

Scenario 3: "What happens if two customers pay 30 days later than usual?"
  A representative pair of concurrent mid-size project draws totals approximately $102,096 based on
  average monthly invoicing in this dataset. Given the minimum cash balance already seen in this dataset
  ($145,615 in 2022-09), a 30-day delay on that amount landing in a similarly tight month would push cash
  meaningfully closer to (or through) the $60,000 operating buffer this business has historically maintained, likely
  requiring a full draw on the $275,000 line of credit that has gone unused across the entire four-year history (see
  Insight #3) -- this is precisely the kind of event the business has had the financing capacity to absorb, but has not
  yet had to actually test.

Scenario 4: "How many additional projects can I safely take on right now?"
  Current field crew capacity, benchmarked at the 2023 peak, supports roughly $4,855,022 of annual
  revenue; 2025 actual revenue was $3,161,381, leaving approximately $1,693,641 of headroom
  (about 54% of current volume) before field labor becomes the binding constraint.
  Translated into projects: that headroom is roughly equivalent to 2-3 additional "Remodel - Small" projects per year, or
  about one additional "Remodel - Large" / partial custom-home slot, without new hires -- consistent with signed backlog
  ($284,252) and weighted pipeline ($879,750) already
  in hand. Taking on meaningfully more than that (e.g., committing to 2 more custom homes simultaneously) without first
  executing Scenario 1's hire would risk the same overhead-doesn't-flex dynamic that produced the 2024 loss in Insight #2,
  compounded by the cash-timing risk in Insight #3.
